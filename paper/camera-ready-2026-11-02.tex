\begin{abstract}
As vision--language--action policies become more computationally demanding,
deployment under explicit resource constraints poses an allocation problem: how
much inference effort should construct each action chunk, and how long should it
execute before prediction is refreshed from a new observation? We study this
question by varying generation effort and execution prefix separately, measuring
both closed-loop success and inference expenditure under a declared counting
boundary, and distinguishing
prespecified comparisons from amended descriptive analyses. We use
one frozen SmolVLA checkpoint in synchronous LIBERO simulation as a worked case,
with denoising count as its refinement control. At a fixed two-action prefix, the
prespecified comparison found 139/160 successes with two denoising steps versus
122/160 with five, while using 5.9\% less counted arithmetic per control step;
82.1\% of generation arithmetic at the default setting was independent of
denoising. An amended descriptive contrast
found 138/160 versus 139/160 successes when executing five rather than two actions
per prediction, with 60.7\% lower arithmetic per attempt; equivalence was not
established. These checkpoint-specific results do not define a general allocation
rule. They show why closed-loop behavior, explicitly bounded inference cost, and the status
of each comparison must be considered together.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Chunked robot policies make two coupled deployment choices. Each policy call
constructs an action sequence, the robot executes only a prefix of that sequence,
and the policy then predicts again from a newer observation. More computation
inside a call may change the predicted actions. Executing a longer prefix reduces
the number of calls, but it also delays feedback and commits the robot to later
actions from an older prediction. The useful setting therefore depends on both
closed-loop behavior and the total cost of prediction over an attempt.

We call adjustable computation spent constructing one action chunk
\emph{refinement}, and obtaining a new observation-conditioned prediction
\emph{refresh}. The number of actions executed before replanning is the
\emph{execution prefix}; it controls how often refresh occurs. We ask: \emph{under
an inference budget, how much computation should construct each chunk, and how
many actions should the robot execute before the policy refreshes it?}

Our evaluation varies the model-specific refinement setting and execution prefix
separately. It counts arithmetic across each policy call under a stated boundary
and the resulting expenditure over complete attempts. Discovery tasks are used
to choose and freeze comparisons; different held-out tasks are then used to
evaluate them. Any
comparison added after outcomes were visible is labelled \emph{amended
descriptive}, so its interpretation remains separate from the prespecified tests.

This comparison is intended for chunked policies with a controllable generation
effort. Depending on the architecture, that control could be integration steps,
candidate generation, search, or verification effort. The present study tests
only denoising count, and numerical control values should not be compared across
architectures. Synchronous execution keeps sequencing and cost boundaries fixed;
the study does not determine an asynchronous schedule, physical-robot latency, or
a hardware-independent optimum.

We use one frozen SmolVLA checkpoint as a worked case study \cite{smolvla}.
Denoising count controls refinement, and the execution prefix controls refresh.
We evaluate 12 configurations in 480 discovery attempts, then seven frozen
settings in 1,120 held-out attempts on eight tasks excluded from selection. Three
denoising comparisons were fixed before the held-out evaluation. Comparisons that
change the execution prefix were added later and remain descriptive because they
change both observation age and the action positions executed from each chunk.

At a fixed two-action prefix, the prespecified primary comparison gives 139/160
successes with two denoising steps and 122/160 with five, while using less counted
arithmetic. At the model's default ten-step setting, 82.1\% of generation
arithmetic is independent of denoising, which limits the savings available from
refinement alone. With two denoising steps, executing five actions per prediction
gives 138/160 successes and uses 60.7\% less arithmetic per attempt than executing
two; the available data do not establish equivalent quality. A simple cost-aware
rule also selects the same settings as the fitted response model at all three
tested budgets, so fitting has no demonstrated selection advantage here.

The contributions are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
    \item a formulation that connects model-specific generation effort and
    execution prefix to nominal compute rate and realized attempt expenditure;
    \item an evaluation procedure that separates discovery, prespecified held-out
    comparisons, and amended descriptive analyses; and
    \item a SmolVLA case study that demonstrates the procedure across 12
    discovery configurations and seven held-out settings.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Allocation under a compute budget.}
Compute-optimal scaling studies ask how a fixed training budget should be divided
between model size and data \cite{chinchilla}; related embodied work studies
scaling in agent pretraining and world models \cite{pearcescaling}. Including
deployment demand can change the preferred allocation \cite{beyondchinchilla}.
These studies establish the need to state the budget and counting boundary. We
instead allocate deployment-time compute within one frozen policy and evaluate
the choice by closed-loop task success.

\paragraph{Computation used to generate an action.}
RoboMonkey spends additional inference compute on action candidates and
verification \cite{robomonkey}. This is one form of refinement: both proposal and
verification cost belong to the policy call. SmolVLA provides the flow-matching
policy used in our case study and also studies asynchronous inference
\cite{smolvla}. VLA-Perf examines how model
architecture, hardware, placement, networking, and asynchronous execution affect
VLA inference performance \cite{vlaperf}. These works show that arithmetic and
elapsed time depend on both the model and its execution system. Our case study
holds the checkpoint, hardware, and synchronous execution path fixed so that the
effects of refinement and execution prefix can be compared within one system.

\paragraph{Choosing when to refresh.}
Adaptive Action Chunking uses action entropy to choose how much of a predicted
chunk to execute \cite{adaptivechunking}. DVAC uses variation observed during
denoising to adapt the execution prefix \cite{dvac}. ElegantVLA schedules
vision--language recomputation, reuse, and action generation during execution
\cite{elegantvla}. These methods change the schedule within an episode. We map
fixed refinement and prefix settings first, separate selection from held-out
evaluation, and report both nominal compute rate and realized attempt cost.
This design evaluates the axes available to an adaptive method; it does not test
whether a fixed schedule is better than adaptation.

\section{Allocating Compute Between Generation and Execution}
\label{sec:allocation}

We compare two deployment choices for a frozen chunked policy: the effort used to
generate one action chunk and the number of actions executed before requesting a
new prediction. We represent refinement by an architecture-specific effort
control $r$ and refresh frequency by the execution prefix $h$. The evaluation
procedure measures both nominal compute rate and
realized attempt expenditure, selects comparisons on discovery tasks,
prespecifies the held-out comparisons, and evaluates them on separate tasks. Success and
cost are reported together. Later analyses remain explicitly descriptive, and
arithmetic savings are kept distinct from latency. The SmolVLA case study
instantiates generation effort with its denoising count.

\subsection{Compute rate and attempt expenditure}

Let $K$ be the predicted action-chunk length, $1\leq h\leq K$ the execution
prefix, and
$r$ the architecture-specific generation-effort control. At each policy query,
the policy predicts $K$ actions, executes only the first $h$, discards the
remaining $K-h$, and generates a new chunk from a new observation. Thus $h$
controls replanning frequency without changing $K$. We reserve $T_e$ for the
number of actions executed in attempt $e$, and write $F_{\mathrm{gen}}(r)$ for the
measured arithmetic of one generation under a stated counting boundary.
The study compares configurations $(h,r)$ within a policy; it does not assume
that numerical values of $r$ are comparable across architectures. Ignoring a
possibly partial final prefix, the nominal compute rate per simulated control
step is
\[
 b(h,r)=\frac{F_{\mathrm{gen}}(r)}{h}.
\]
Let $p_{\mathrm{success}}(h,r)$ denote expected closed-loop success under the
specified task distribution.
For a candidate set $\mathcal C$ and budget ceiling $B$, the allocation target is
\begin{equation}
 (h^\star,r^\star)(B)\in
 \arg\max_{(h,r)\in\mathcal C:\,b(h,r)\le B}
 p_{\mathrm{success}}(h,r).
 \label{eq:allocation}
\end{equation}
A configuration need not exhaust the ceiling. This is a deployment compute-rate
constraint for a fixed checkpoint, rather than a training-plus-deployment
objective. The equation defines the allocation decision; the experiments below
estimate its unknown success term from paired rollouts.

In the SmolVLA instantiation, $K=50$ and $r=d$, where $d$ is the denoising
count. Figure~\ref{fig:closed-loop-policy} shows this case-study execution loop.
Each policy call predicts a fixed-length chunk, executes only the first $h$
actions, discards the remainder, and refreshes from the resulting observation.
The denoising count controls generation effort inside the policy call, while $h$
controls how frequently the policy receives a new observation.

\caption{Closed-loop execution in the SmolVLA case study. Each policy call
predicts $K$ actions after $d$ denoising steps, executes the first $h$, discards

\label{fig:closed-loop-policy}
\end{figure}

We distinguish this rate from total arithmetic per attempted episode. In the
case study, generation cost is fixed for a given $r$. If attempt $e$ executes
$T_e$ actions, its generated-chunk expenditure is
\begin{equation}
 C_e=\left\lceil T_e/h\right\rceil F_{\mathrm{gen}}(r).
 \label{eq:episode-cost}
\end{equation}
The final generated chunk is charged even if only part of its execution prefix
is used. If generation cost varies across calls, as it can for early-exit,
sampling, search, or verification procedures, attempt expenditure must instead
sum the measured cost of every call. All-attempt means include unsuccessful
episodes. Different trajectories and termination times can therefore change
expenditure at the same nominal rate. FLOPs, host elapsed time, and CUDA-stream
time retain separate units and measurement boundaries.

\subsection{A fixed-cost floor in the SmolVLA implementation}
\label{sec:results}

For the case-study checkpoint and reference input, generation cost separates
into a denoising-independent term and a term that grows linearly with $d$. To
rounding, the measured arithmetic is
\[
 F_{\mathrm{gen}}(d)\simeq 527.108+11.457d\quad\text{GFLOPs}
\]
at all six calibrated denoising settings. Native generation at $d=10$ costs
641.675 GFLOPs, of which 82.1\% is independent of $d$. Halving denoising from
ten to five saves 8.9\% of counted generation work; reducing it to two saves
14.3\%. Equivalently, relative to native deployment $(h,d)=(1,10)$,
\[
 \frac{b(h,d)}{b_{\mathrm{native}}}
 \simeq \frac{0.821+0.179(d/10)}{h}.
\]
Here $b_{\mathrm{native}}=b(1,10)$.

Profiling counts matrix products, convolutions, and attention across vision,
language prefill, and the action expert, using two FLOPs per multiply-add.
Embedding lookups, normalization, activations, elementwise arithmetic, and
external preprocessing are excluded. We therefore refer to \emph{dense neural
arithmetic}, rather than every scalar operation. Calibration uses one real
development observation and fixed tensor shapes; Appendix~\ref{app:generation-calibration}
gives the procedure.

We prespecify compute-rate ceilings of $0.25$, $0.50$, and $1.00$ times native.
Eligibility uses measured per-$d$ values, not the rounded affine expression.
For example, $(1,2)$ costs 0.857 times native rate and is ineligible at half-native
budget, whereas $(2,2)$ costs 0.429 times native. The denoising-independent work
alone excludes $h=1$ at half-native budget and $h\leq2$ at quarter-native budget.
A tighter budget can therefore force a longer execution prefix before any
quality-based choice is made. This is an implementation-specific arithmetic
floor under the declared counting boundary, not a universal VLA scaling law.

Calibration uses a fixed input shape. A separate audit using the language lengths
observed in the tasks leaves eligibility unchanged under the same absolute
ceilings (Appendix~\ref{app:shape-audit}). The reference-input arithmetic used
for selection remains distinct from task-specific arithmetic and elapsed time.
On the fixed calibration input, reducing $d$ from 10 to 5 lowers measured host
generation time by 40.5\% while counted arithmetic falls by 8.9\%
(Appendix~\ref{app:fixed-timing}); FLOPs are therefore not a latency proxy. The
cost model determines which settings satisfy a ceiling. The following experiments
test their closed-loop outcomes.

\section{SmolVLA Case Study}
\label{sec:method}

This section applies the allocation procedure to one frozen checkpoint. Discovery
maps the candidate settings and freezes the comparisons; confirmation then tests
those comparisons on tasks excluded from selection.

\subsection{Discovery and confirmation design}

We call evaluation on tasks excluded from selection the \emph{held-out
confirmation phase}, or confirmation for short.

We evaluate a public SmolVLA checkpoint on LIBERO \cite{libero}. The predicted
chunk length is fixed at $K=50$, and the model's native deployment uses
$(h,d)=(1,10)$. We hold the checkpoint, observations, preprocessing, action
normalization, control period, and task-specific episode horizons fixed.

The discovery study varies $h\in\{1,2,5\}$ and
$d\in\{2,5,10,20\}$ on four Spatial tasks. Each task has five initial states and
two rollout seeds, giving 40 paired task--state--seed identities. Every identity
receives all twelve settings in randomized order, for 480 attempts. Two discovery
tasks overlap pre-study pilot work; the other two were chosen before their study
outcomes were observed. This partial prior exposure is part of the reason that
discovery is used for selection rather than final evaluation.

After discovery, we freeze three denoising comparisons, one at each compute
ceiling. Their selected settings and comparators, plus native deployment, produce
seven distinct settings. Confirmation evaluates all seven on eight Goal and
Object tasks excluded from selection. Each task has ten initial states and two
rollout seeds, giving 160 attempts per setting and 1,120 in total. Prior exposure
to these tasks during unrelated development is not assessed. No equivalence
margin was prespecified, so similar observed success cannot establish equivalent
quality. Appendix~\ref{app:analysis-provenance} records which comparisons were
frozen and which were added later as descriptive analyses.

Conditions are paired by initial state and rollout seed. They also share policy
noise while their queries occur at the same simulation step. Every denoising
setting traverses the same integration interval; changing $d$ changes only its
discretization. Internal checks cover initial conditions, generated and executed
actions, and recorded outcomes. They do not provide an independent simulator
replay; Appendix~\ref{app:verification} states the validation boundary.

Success gives equal weight to each task; because every task has the same number
of states and seeds, this equals the overall success fraction. Descriptive 95\%
intervals use 20,000 bootstrap draws of states within the fixed tasks while
retaining paired seeds and settings. The intervals condition on the four
discovery tasks or eight confirmation tasks, and secondary intervals are not
adjusted for multiple comparisons. Arithmetic includes every generated chunk,
including failed attempts and partly consumed final prefixes. Appendix~\ref{app:fixed-timing}
reports timing separately.

\subsection{Discovery: observed success is not monotonic in refinement}
\label{sec:discovery-quality}

Native $(1,10)$ succeeds in 25/40 discovery attempts. The highest observed count
is 28/40 for $(1,5)$; $(1,2)$ and $(2,2)$ each succeed in 26/40. At $h=5$,
success falls from 21/40 to 14/40 as $d$ increases from 2 to 20, with intermediate
counts of 18/40 and 16/40.
Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:frontier} shows every configuration.
These point estimates do not justify imposing monotonic improvement with
denoising. The small paired sample also does not certify equal quality for
configurations with similar observed counts.

Selection also depends on how cost is defined. Nominal arithmetic rate differs
from realized attempt expenditure, and arithmetic savings differ from elapsed
time. Replacing the arithmetic ceiling with a host-time ceiling changes which
prefixes are feasible. This sensitivity analysis does not alter the frozen
arithmetic-based experiment. Appendix~\ref{app:discovery-outcomes} gives the
full discovery results, and Appendix~\ref{app:timing-sensitivity} gives the
resource-coordinate analysis.

\subsection{Selection rules and prespecified comparisons}
\label{sec:prediction}

Discovery freezes $(5,2)$, $(2,2)$, and $(1,2)$ at the quarter-, half-, and
full-native ceilings. Their comparators are $(5,5)$, $(2,5)$, and $(1,5)$,
respectively; $(2,2)$ versus $(2,5)$ is the primary comparison. Every selected
setting is an observed discovery-grid point, so confirmation tests performance
on new tasks rather than extrapolation to an untested setting. Each pair changes
denoising at a fixed prefix. The pairs share a compute ceiling but do not have
equal compute rates, so they test the selected setting against a feasible
alternative rather than directly dividing a fixed amount between refinement and
refresh.

At the primary ceiling, $(2,2)$ has the highest eligible discovery success. Its
comparator $(2,5)$ ties with $(2,10)$ for the highest count among the remaining
eligible settings and is chosen by the lower-cost tie-break. A logistic response
model fitted only to discovery data produces all three frozen selections. A
simpler rule---use the fewest denoising steps, then the shortest feasible
prefix---produces the same selections. Because the simple rule was documented
after data collection began, this agreement is descriptive. Confirmation cannot
show that fitting improves selection when both procedures choose the same
settings. Appendix~\ref{app:selectors} contains the model specification,
candidate set, and diagnostics.

\subsection{Prespecified refinement comparisons}

The primary comparison holds $h=2$ fixed. Reducing denoising from $d=5$ to
$d=2$ raises observed success from 122/160 to 139/160.
The paired difference is $+10.6$ percentage points, with a task-conditional 95\%
interval of $[+4.4,+16.9]$. It also reduces nominal reference arithmetic per
control step by 5.9\%, from 292.2 to 275.0 GFLOPs. For this checkpoint and prefix,
less refinement therefore improves observed success while reducing counted work.
\par\end{samepage}

Mean arithmetic per attempt falls by 15.6\%, from 48.7 to 41.1 TFLOPs. This
attempt-level difference also reflects the changed trajectories: mean episode
length is 149.0 control steps for $(2,2)$ and 166.3 for $(2,5)$. The effect is
positive on seven tasks and tied on one. Across the 160 paired identities,
$(2,2)$ has 24 wins, seven losses, and 129 ties.
Appendix~\ref{app:primary-costs} reports task sensitivity and
expenditure intervals. Counted arithmetic does not establish a physical-latency
improvement.

The other two prespecified comparisons are smaller and uncertain. At $h=5$,
$d=2$ gives 138/160 successes and $d=5$ gives 133/160. At $h=1$, the counts are
137/160 and 136/160. Both point estimates favor $d=2$, but both intervals include
zero (Figure~\ref{fig:success-compute}). The evidence therefore supports the
primary result at $h=2$, not a uniform benefit across execution prefixes. The
observed $+10.6$-point effect is also larger than the response model's frozen

effect-size prediction.

\subsection{Amended descriptive execution-prefix comparisons}

These comparisons hold denoising fixed and vary how many actions are executed
before replanning. Because they were added after outcomes were visible, they
describe observed trade-offs rather than test prespecified hypotheses.
Appendix~\ref{app:confirmation-details} gives their provenance and two additional
contrasts involving native $(1,10)$.

At $d=2$, $(2,2)$ exceeds $(5,2)$ by just one success: 139/160 versus 138/160,
a paired difference of $+0.6$ percentage points with 95\% interval
$[-6.3,+6.9]$. Executing five actions per prediction, $(5,2)$, uses 60\% less
nominal arithmetic per control step and 60.7\% less mean arithmetic per attempt:
16.1 versus 41.1 TFLOPs. The interval permits meaningful success differences in
either direction, so the observed counts do not establish equivalent quality.
The aggregate also hides variation: $(2,2)-(5,2)$ is positive on three tasks,
negative on three, and tied on two (Table~\ref{tab:confirmation-tasks}).

Refreshing every action with $(1,2)$ instead of every two actions gives two
fewer successes, a difference of $-1.3$ points with 95\% interval
$[-7.5,+5.0]$, at 83.3 versus 41.1 TFLOPs per attempt. More frequent refresh has no demonstrated quality
benefit in these SmolVLA contrasts. Native-relative comparisons, which also change
denoising, are reported separately in Appendix~\ref{app:confirmation-details}.

Discovery point estimates favor shorter prefixes at $d=2$
(Section~\ref{sec:discovery-quality}), but confirmation does not establish that
advantage on the held-out tasks. Because the two phases use different task sets,
their different prefix patterns cannot be attributed to task family without a
shared-task test. The cost model determines feasibility, not the quality ranking
on new tasks.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}

The experiments expose two limits on inference allocation. First, reducing a
model's adjustable refinement work may save little when most generation cost is
fixed. Second, executing more actions from each chunk can reduce attempt cost
substantially, but it also delays feedback and changes which predicted action
positions reach the environment. Cost and closed-loop success must therefore be
measured together rather than inferred from a single per-call setting.

The SmolVLA case study demonstrates both points. At $h=2$, reducing denoising
from five steps to two improved held-out success by 10.6 percentage points and
reduced nominal counted arithmetic by 5.9\%. This result is specific to that
prefix: the other two prespecified comparisons did not establish a uniform benefit.
Meanwhile, 82.1\% of native generation arithmetic was independent of denoising.
Changing the execution prefix had a much larger cost effect: $(5,2)$ used 60.7\%
less arithmetic per attempt than $(2,2)$ and produced 138 rather than 139
successes, but the study was not designed to establish equivalent quality. The
fitted response model also selected the same settings as a simple cost-aware
rule, so fitting has no demonstrated selection advantage here.

The transferable contribution is an evaluation procedure, rather than a
universal setting. Within each model, define its adjustable generation effort,
measure policy-call cost under an explicit boundary, vary the execution prefix,
freeze comparisons before held-out evaluation, and report realized attempt
expenditure with success. Generation effort can represent integration steps,
candidate sampling, search, or verification, provided its cost is measured. When
comparing architectures, the meaningful evidence is each model's within-model
allocation result; unlike control values and counting boundaries should not be
pooled directly.

\paragraph{Scope and interpretation.}
Neither control isolates a single mechanism. Changing denoising changes numerical
integration, while changing the prefix changes both observation age and executed
action positions. The primary mechanism therefore remains unresolved. The study
uses one checkpoint, twelve tasks, and synchronous simulation. Its intervals are
conditional on the sampled tasks, secondary contrasts are unadjusted, and the
four discovery tasks provide limited task-level validation. Prefix comparisons
were added after outcomes were visible and remain descriptive. Two denoising
steps is the lowest tested setting, and no equivalence margin was prespecified.

\paragraph{Measurement limits.}
The arithmetic count excludes some operation classes and is not a latency proxy;
synchronous simulator steps do not establish real-time robot performance. The
recorded outcomes pass internal source and consistency checks, but the full
confirmation trajectories were not replayed in an independent, version-matched
simulator. The result is therefore supported by strong internal validation rather
than independent outcome reproduction.

\paragraph{Next experiments.}
The first priority is independent replay of the retained primary trajectories.
A predeclared quality-preservation study should then test the cheaper $(5,2)$
setting. A controlled stale-input experiment is needed to separate observation
freshness from action-position effects when $h$ changes. Finally, the same
within-model design should be repeated across checkpoints, policy architectures,
benchmark families, and asynchronous or physical-robot execution. Those studies
can determine when additional generation work is worth paying for and when the
budget is better spent producing a new chunk from fresher feedback.

\subsection{Resource-coordinate sensitivity}
\label{app:timing-sensitivity}

Arithmetic savings and elapsed savings differ. Mean rollout time is 111.9 seconds
per attempt for native deployment, 61.8 seconds for $(1,5)$, and 21.8 seconds for
$(2,2)$. Their mean generated-chunk expenditures are 109.6, 91.7, and 45.0 TFLOPs,
respectively. These are observed all-attempt means under the same task allocation;
they include different trajectories and outcomes. Table~\ref{tab:resources}
reports all discovery configurations.

There is also an ordering reversal: $(1,5)$ uses more arithmetic per attempt
than $(2,20)$ (91.7 versus 67.8 TFLOPs), but less rollout time (61.8 versus
107.8 seconds). These means include different trajectories. On a fixed input,
reducing $d$ from 10 to 5 lowers mean generation host time from 629.6 to
374.3 ms (40.5\%), despite only 8.9\% less counted arithmetic. The calibration
therefore also separates arithmetic savings from elapsed savings without
trajectory differences. It does not resolve their component-level cause.
A FLOP-constrained choice is not necessarily a latency- or throughput-optimal
choice.

As a descriptive sensitivity analysis, define calibrated host-time rate
$b_T(h,d)=\overline{T}_{\mathrm{gen}}(d)/h$, the amortized fixed-input generation
time per simulated control step, and normalize it by native host-time rate.
For $(1,2)$, $(2,2)$, and $(5,2)$, the normalized arithmetic rates are
0.857, 0.429, and 0.171, while the normalized host-time rates are 0.346, 0.173,
and 0.069. Consequently, $(1,2)$ is ineligible at a half-native arithmetic
ceiling but eligible at a separately normalized half-native host-time ceiling.
These resource constraints admit different refresh schedules; the choices do
not establish quality optima. The frozen experiment remains arithmetic-based.

\section{Frozen Selection and Diagnostic Analyses}
\label{app:selectors}

\subsection{Frozen selection procedure}

We compare two logistic response-model families. A compute-only model uses a
quadratic in log normalized rate. An allocation model uses $\log h$,
$\log(d/10)$, both squared terms, and their interaction. Each family is fitted
with ridge penalty 1 or 10. Leave-one-task-out log loss selects the model, which
is then refitted on the discovery set. No individual seed repeat is held out
while another repeat from the same task remains in training.

The candidate set is $h\in\{1,2,3,5\}$ crossed with $d\in\{2,3,5,8,10,20\}$:
the 12 discovery configurations and 12 untested configurations. Their calibrated costs determine
eligibility; the fitted model selects the highest predicted success under each
ceiling. Ties favor lower compute, then lexicographic order.

\subsection{Selector diagnostics}

The simple rule minimizes denoising count, then chooses the shortest feasible
prefix. It reproduces every learned recommendation (Table~\ref{tab:selectors});
confirmation outcomes cannot distinguish these selectors at the tested budgets.
The compute-only choices use the original ridge-10 fit. Its quarter- and
half-budget choices are absent from the confirmation plan, so this evaluation
cannot compare those decisions on held-out tasks.

The selected allocation model has log loss 0.7048, compared with 0.7067 for the
best compute-only model. This small difference provides no evidence of an added
predictive advantage. Under the same mean natural-log binary cross-entropy,
constant $p=0.5$ scores 0.6931 and an intercept-only model fitted separately on
each training fold scores 0.7170. The response-model probabilities should
therefore not be read as calibrated held-out-task forecasts. The primary test
evaluates a recommendation shared with simpler selectors, so it does not
establish an additional benefit from model-based ranking.
